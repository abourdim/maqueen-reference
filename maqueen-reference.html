§ 01

What is it, exactly?

The Maqueen Lite (DFRobot SKU ROB0148) is a palm-sized programmable robot built around the BBC micro:bit. It's a chassis with two motors, a handful of sensors, and a slot for the micro:bit to plug into. The micro:bit is the brain; the chassis is the body. You write code in a browser, drop it onto the micro:bit, slide it into the robot, and the robot does what you said.

It is not a remote-controlled toy and not a kit you build from scratch. It arrives assembled, ready in five minutes. What changes between sessions is the code — the same hardware can be a line-follower, a Morse-code blinker, a maze solver, an autonomous sumo bot, or a node in a swarm.

The Lite is the entry point. There is a heavier sibling (Maqueen Plus V2) and a family of clip-on mechanical kits (forklift, gripper, bulldozer). All share the same programming model.

§ 02

DFRobot has produced four main Maqueen models since 2018. They share the micro:bit slot and most of the API, but differ in motors, batteries, and onboard sensors. Pick the one that matches the budget and the activities you want to run.

§ 03

Why Maqueen?

Educational robots are crowded territory. LEGO Mindstorms, Sphero, Ozobot, mBot, Edison, VEX IQ — the shelf is full. The Maqueen earns its place on three specific axes:

Cost-to-capability ratio

At ≈ €30 the Lite is roughly one twelfth the price of a Mindstorms set, yet exposes line sensing, distance sensing, IR remote, RGB lighting, sound (v2), two servo outputs, and an I²C bus for expansion. A class set of 15 robots fits in the budget of one or two LEGO kits.

Ecosystem openness

The brain is a BBC micro:bit — an open-hardware educational board with first-party browser tools, no install, no account, free forever. Code is written in MakeCode (Blocks → JavaScript transition built in) or MicroPython. The Maqueen extension pxt-maqueen is open-source on GitHub. Nothing is locked, nothing expires, nothing requires a subscription.

Pedagogical depth

The robot grows with the student. A 9-year-old can blink an LED in three blocks; a 14-year-old can implement a Q-learning agent that learns to navigate a maze; a sixteen-year-old can train a sound-classification model in CreateAI and deploy it onboard. Same robot, same software, six years of progression.

Where Maqueen falls short

Be honest with students: the Lite has no encoders, so dead-reckoning beyond ~30 cm is unreliable. Battery sag from alkalines noticeably affects motor speed mid-session. The micro:bit v1 has no microphone — for any sound-based or AI activity, v2 is mandatory.

§ 04

What's onboard

A Maqueen Lite straight out of the box, before any expansion, gives you eleven addressable things. Memorise this list — every activity in the canon is some combination of these.

  • Two TT-style geared DC motors with all-metal pinion gears. Driven through an onboard motor controller chip on I²C address 0x10 (so the API hides the wiring entirely — you say "motor left, forward, speed 150" and it happens).
  • Two front-facing white LEDs (P8 left, P12 right). Headlights, indicators, eyes — pick a metaphor.
  • Four WS2812B RGB NeoPixels arranged around the perimeter, all daisy-chained on P15. Full-colour underglow, animations, status indicators.
  • Two downward-facing IR line sensors at the front (P13 left, P14 right). Returns 0 over white surfaces, 1 over black — the heart of every line-following activity.
  • One IR receiver on P16, compatible with cheap NEC-protocol remotes. Each remote button maps to a numeric code.
  • One passive buzzer on P0 (the standard micro:bit pin for music). Plays tones, tunes, rhythm patterns.
  • One ultrasonic socket at the front for an HC-SR04 (sold separately or as part of a bundle). Reads distance in centimetres via maqueen.Ultrasonic().
  • Two servo outputs, S1 on micro:bit pin P1, S2 on P2. 0–180° standard hobby servos. Used by all four Mechanic kits.
  • One exposed I²C bus (SDA on P20, SCL on P19) for expansion: HuskyLens, OLED screens, additional sensors.
  • One battery holder for 3 × AAA cells, with a slide power switch. Nominal 4.5 V, working range roughly 3.6–5 V.
  • One micro:bit edge connector at the front. Either v1 or v2 fits; v2 unlocks the onboard microphone, speaker, and touch logo.

Free pins matter

P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10, P11 are not used by any onboard Maqueen Lite component. They are exposed on the micro:bit edge and on the expansion screw terminals — that's where you wire your own sensor or actuator.

§ 07

Getting started — first run in 5 minutes

From the box on your desk to a robot moving on the floor: roughly five minutes if everything works, ten if you hit the typical hiccups.

  1. Insert 3 × AAA batteries into the underside holder. Mind the polarity. Alkaline or NiMH both work; NiMH gives more consistent motor speed across a session.
  2. Slot the micro:bit into the front edge connector. The 25-LED matrix faces forward, the gold pads on the connector go in first.
  3. Open makecode.microbit.org in any browser. No account needed.
  4. Add the maqueen extension: gear icon → Extensions → search "maqueen" → click the DFRobot result. The block palette gains a "Maqueen" category.
  5. If you want RGB: also add the neopixel extension the same way.
  6. Drop in three blocks: on start → maqueen motor All forward speed 100 → pause 1000 ms → maqueen motor stop. That's a robot that scoots forward for one second.
  7. Click Download. A .hex file lands in your Downloads folder.
  8. Plug the micro:bit into your computer via USB. It mounts as a USB drive called MICROBIT. Drag the .hex onto it. The yellow LED on the back blinks while flashing, then stops.
  9. Unplug, place the robot on the floor, flip the power switch. It moves.
§ 08

Programming environments

The same robot accepts code from at least four different toolchains. Pick by audience.

Default · ages 8+

MakeCode

Microsoft's browser editor. Drag-and-drop blocks, with a one-click toggle to JavaScript and Python. The Maqueen extension is first-class. makecode.microbit.org

Ages 12+

Python (MicroPython)

The micro:bit Foundation's editor. Pure text, real Python. Maqueen access via I²C / pin-level calls. python.microbit.org

Desktop · multilingual

Mind+

DFRobot's own editor. Scratch-style blocks plus a real-mode that compiles to Arduino C++ and Python. Useful when school computers are offline. mindplus.cc

AI · v2 only

CreateAI

Train a tiny model (gestures, sounds) in the browser using the micro:bit's own sensors, export as a MakeCode extension. createai.microbit.org

For classrooms transitioning from Scratch, MakeCode is the painless step up. The Blocks→JavaScript toggle is the single best feature in the whole stack: a child writes blocks, then sees the same logic as text, then starts typing it directly.

§ 09

The maqueen API at a glance

The pxt-maqueen extension exposes one namespace: maqueen. Everything onboard is accessed through it. Below, the eight calls that cover ~90% of activities.

Movement

JavaScript · motors
// Run both motors clockwise at speed 150 (range 0-255)
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 150)

// Stop everything
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)

// Spin in place: left back, right forward
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M1, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 120)
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M2, maqueen.Dir.CW,  120)

Front white LEDs

JavaScript · LEDs
maqueen.writeLED(maqueen.LED.LEDLeft,  maqueen.LEDswitch.turnOn)
maqueen.writeLED(maqueen.LED.LEDRight, maqueen.LEDswitch.turnOff)

Line sensors

JavaScript · line tracking
let left  = maqueen.readPatrol(maqueen.Patrol.PatrolLeft)
let right = maqueen.readPatrol(maqueen.Patrol.PatrolRight)
// Returns 0 over white, 1 over black

Ultrasonic distance

JavaScript · SR04
let cm = maqueen.Ultrasonic()  // returns centimetres, 0-400
if (cm < 15 && cm > 0) {
  basic.showIcon(IconNames.No)
}

Servos

JavaScript · servos
maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, 90)   // 0-180 deg; keep 10-170 for safety
maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S2, 45)

RGB NeoPixels (separate neopixel extension)

JavaScript · RGB
let strip = neopixel.create(DigitalPin.P15, 4, NeoPixelMode.RGB)
strip.showColor(neopixel.colors(NeoPixelColors.Purple))
basic.pause(500)
strip.clear()
strip.show()

IR remote

JavaScript · IR receiver
let code = maqueen.IR_Read(IR_Pin.P16)
if (code == 0x45) basic.showString("PWR")

§ 10

Code recipes — six things that always work

Drop these into a fresh MakeCode project. Each is self-contained and tested against the working API.

1. Square dance

Drive a 30 cm × 30 cm square. Tweak the timing constants for your battery level.

JavaScript
for (let i = 0; i < 4; i++) {
  maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 120)
  basic.pause(800)
  maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M1, maqueen.Dir.CW,  120)
  maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M2, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 120)
  basic.pause(450)
}
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)

2. Two-sensor line follower

The simplest viable line-follow: read both, choose one of four cases.

JavaScript
basic.forever(function () {
  let L = maqueen.readPatrol(maqueen.Patrol.PatrolLeft)
  let R = maqueen.readPatrol(maqueen.Patrol.PatrolRight)
  if (L == 0 && R == 0) {           // both white — drive straight
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 100)
  } else if (L == 1 && R == 0) {    // drifting right — turn left
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M1, maqueen.Dir.CW, 40)
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M2, maqueen.Dir.CW, 120)
  } else if (L == 0 && R == 1) {    // drifting left — turn right
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M1, maqueen.Dir.CW, 120)
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M2, maqueen.Dir.CW, 40)
  } else {                              // both black — intersection or end
    maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
  }
})

3. Obstacle avoider

Forward until something appears within 15 cm, then turn away and resume.

JavaScript
basic.forever(function () {
  let d = maqueen.Ultrasonic()
  if (d > 0 && d < 15) {
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 100)
    basic.pause(300)
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M1, maqueen.Dir.CW,  120)
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M2, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 120)
    basic.pause(400)
  } else {
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 110)
  }
})

4. Distance-to-colour gauge

The four RGB pixels glow green-amber-red as something gets closer. Useful as a tangible explanation of how analog sensors map to outputs.

JavaScript
let strip = neopixel.create(DigitalPin.P15, 4, NeoPixelMode.RGB)
basic.forever(function () {
  let d = maqueen.Ultrasonic()
  if (d > 30) strip.showColor(neopixel.colors(NeoPixelColors.Green))
  else if (d > 10) strip.showColor(neopixel.colors(NeoPixelColors.Yellow))
  else strip.showColor(neopixel.colors(NeoPixelColors.Red))
  basic.pause(100)
})

5. Clap-to-go (v2 only)

Uses the v2 onboard microphone. The robot lurches forward when it hears a sharp sound.

JavaScript
input.onSound(DetectedSound.Loud, function () {
  maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 160)
  basic.pause(600)
  maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
})

6. Same thing in Python

Square dance, but in MicroPython, for the older students. Note the Python API uses different verbs — wrap them in helper functions early.

Python · MicroPython
from microbit import *
import maqueen

for _ in range(4):
    maqueen.motor_run(maqueen.ALL, maqueen.CW, 120)
    sleep(800)
    maqueen.motor_run(maqueen.M1, maqueen.CW,  120)
    maqueen.motor_run(maqueen.M2, maqueen.CCW, 120)
    sleep(450)

maqueen.motor_stop(maqueen.ALL)

Reading order — what to teach when

The wiki lists the examples in catalog order, not pedagogical order. A pragmatic teaching sequence:

  1. Setup & smoke test: #1 (calibration) → #7 (LED flash) → #16 (get product info). At the end of this trio the student has confirmed the upload pipeline, the chassis power, and the firmware identity.
  2. Single-component reads: #10 (line sensor raw) → #6 (ultrasonic raw) → #8 (IR key codes) → #13 (light level). One sensor at a time, value displayed on the matrix. Builds intuition for what "reading a sensor" actually means.
  3. Single-component drives: #4 (motors) → #15 (servo) → #5 (RGB ambient). One actuator at a time.
  4. Combined behaviours: #11 (ultrasonic obstacle avoidance) → #3 / #12 (line following) → #2 / #9 (IR-driven Maqueen) → #14 (wireless GamePad).

§ 12

Mechanic add-ons — full feature reference

Each kit is a metal-gear servo plus a mechanical assembly that bolts onto the standard expansion holes on the top deck. Wire the servo to S1 (or S2), code the angle, watch the thing move. Below: shared specs, three universal rules, and a full profile for each of the four kits — what it does, how it moves, how to drive it, and what to teach with it.

Shared specifications

All four kits in the Mechanic family use an identical metal-gear servo and identical mounting hardware:

  • Servo: 12 g ± 1 g, metal-gear, 0–180° control range (operate within 10°–170°), standby current < 45 mA, draws peak current well within Maqueen Lite's 4.5 V rail.
  • Frame: aluminium alloy, sandblasted and oxidised — durable enough for daily classroom use; the moving assemblies on each kit are metal too, not plastic.
  • Control ports: S1 (=P1) or S2 (=P2) on Maqueen Lite. Maqueen Plus models also expose S3.
  • Compatibility: Maqueen 4.0 and later. The Lite that ships today is fully compatible.
  • Bundle: all four kits available together as ROB0163.

Three universal mounting rules

  • Centre the servo at 90° before mounting the moving arm. Otherwise the assembly hits a hard stop and the gears strip.
  • Operate within 10°–170°. DFRobot's own warning. Never command 0 or 180 as a working angle.
  • Calibrate "open" and "closed" (or "up" and "down") angles per build — store them as constants at the top of every program. Servos vary; tolerances vary.

Generic skeleton (works for all four kits)

JavaScript · base template
// Constants — calibrate per kit and per build
const ANGLE_DOWN = 20      // e.g. fork at floor / gripper open
const ANGLE_UP   = 160     // e.g. fork raised / gripper closed

input.onButtonPressed(Button.A, function () {

Universal hardware reference

Before opening any single kit, a few facts apply to all four. Where do they bolt on? Which way does the servo cable face? What tools do you need? This subsection shows the chassis from above with the four standard expansion holes highlighted, the standard servo cable colour code, and the order to do things in.

Mounting points (left) and servo cable wiring (right) — common to all four kits

Plugging in the servo cable

The kit comes with a servo. The servo has a cable. The cable has three coloured wires (orange, red, brown) ending in a small plug. That plug pushes onto three little pins on the chassis labelled S1 (or S2). Here's exactly what it looks like:

S (signal) red wire → goes to + (5 V power) brown wire → goes to − (ground / chassis edge) ✓ RIGHT — kit moves Brown on the OUTSIDE, orange toward the middle. ✗ WRONG — kit will not move Cable flipped. No damage — just pull off, rotate 180°, retry.
Servo connection · brown wire matches the chassis-edge pin labelled −

The 30-second instruction: hold the cable plug in front of the chassis sockets. Match the colours: orange goes to the inside pin, red to the middle, brown to the outside. Push it on gently. If the kit doesn't move when you run code, the plug is on backwards — pull it off, turn it around, push it back. You can't break anything by getting it wrong.

Plugging in the SR04 ultrasonic sensor

If your kit needs distance-sensing (the Push kit, or any obstacle-avoidance activity), you'll be plugging in the SR04 — the small board with two round "eyes" on it. The eyes are how it sees: one sends a pulse of sound, the other listens for the echo. The board has four pins on the bottom that slide into a matching socket on the front of the chassis.

Pins above ↓ line up with holes below · same labels in the same order

The 30-second instruction: hold the SR04 with the two round eyes pointing forwards (same direction the robot drives). Line up the four pins with the four holes on the chassis socket. Push down gently. It should slide on without any force. If you have to push hard, you've got it backwards — lift it straight up, turn it 180°, and try again. You will not break it.

If it still doesn't work after both cables are in

Three things to check, in order: (1) Is the chassis power switch ON? The micro:bit lights up from USB even when the switch is off — but the motors and servo need the chassis switch. (2) Are batteries fresh? Below ≈ 3.6 V the servo gets sluggish and the motors slow down. (3) Did you centre the servo at 90° before attaching the moving arm? If not, the arm is fighting a hard mechanical stop — power off, detach the arm, re-centre, re-attach.

Tools you need (the same every time)

  • Phillips / cross-head screwdriver, small (PH00 or PH0 size). One ships in each kit but a workshop screwdriver is faster.
  • The micro:bit with a known-working program loaded — you'll use it to centre the servo at 90° before mounting. A "blink and hold S1 at 90" 5-line program is enough.
  • The chassis itself, batteries removed and power switch off.
  • About 15 minutes for a first-time build, 5 minutes once you've done it.

Pre-flight checklist (do this once, applies to all four kits)

  1. Power off completely. Slide switch off, remove the AAA batteries. Mounting kits with the chassis live is how gears get stripped.
  2. Remove the micro:bit from its slot. You'll re-insert it after the kit is bolted on, with a centring program ready to flash.
  3. Identify the four M3 expansion holes on the top deck (terracotta circles in the diagram above). They form a small rectangular pattern around the servo terminal area.
  4. Centre the servo at 90° before fixing the moving arm. Re-insert the micro:bit, run a one-line program maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, 90), watch the servo settle, then power off again. Now attach the kit's arm/jaw/mast onto the spline.
  5. Connect the servo cable to S1 (or S2), brown wire toward the chassis edge. If the kit doesn't move when you run code, this is the first thing to flip.
  6. Test the full range slowly with hand-held angles (e.g. servoRun(S1, 30), then servoRun(S1, 150)) before committing the kit to a real program. Listen for the servo buzzing — that's the sign you've gone past a mechanical limit.

🍴 Forklift — ROB0156-F · 📄 Tutorial PDF

Forklift profile · arrow shows fork travel range, terracotta = active servo + linkage

What's in the box

DFRobot's official shipping list for ROB0156-F:

  • 1 × 9 g Metal Gear Servo (with output horn)
  • 1 × Metal Arm Servo Base
  • 1 × Metal Arm Baseplate
  • 3 × Metal Arm Linkages (drive the fork plate)
  • 1 × Metal Arm Plate
  • 1 × Metal Servo Arm Linkage
  • 1 × Metal Forklift Plate (the part that does the lifting)
  • 2 × M3 × 15 mm copper pillars (the mast structure)
  • 5 × M2.5 × 5 mm screws
  • 18 × M3 × 5 mm screws
  • 1 × small Phillips screwdriver

Assembly — step by step

  1. Complete the pre-flight checklist above (power off, micro:bit out, servo centred at 90°, then power off again).
  2. Bolt the servo to the metal connection plate using the two small screws that come pre-fitted in the servo body. Orientation: servo spline shaft points upward when the plate is laid flat.
  3. Bolt the connection plate to the chassis top deck through the four M3 expansion holes (terracotta circles in the universal diagram). The plate determines orientation — there is only one correct way; if it doesn't sit flat, flip it.
  4. Slide the upper rotating arm onto the servo spline. The spline is splined (28 teeth on a typical 12 g metal servo) — push straight down, do not rotate the servo. Once seated, secure with the small horn screw through the centre.
  5. Bolt the vertical mast in front of the servo plate. The mast carries the linkage pivots; it does not move during operation.
  6. Connect the two linkage arms between the upper rotating arm (top) and the fork plate (bottom). The pin-jointed pivots should rotate freely; if they bind, loosen by half a turn.
  7. Bolt the fork plate to the lower ends of the linkage arms. The plate should sit horizontal when the rotating arm is at 90°.
  8. Route the servo cable across the top of the chassis and into S1 (or S2). Brown wire faces the chassis edge.
  9. Re-insert the micro:bit, install batteries, power on.
  10. Verify with hand-tested angles: servoRun(S1, 90) should hold the fork at mid-height. servoRun(S1, 35) drops it to the ground. servoRun(S1, 140) raises it. If any of these strain the servo, calibrate (next section) before going further.

Mechanism. A vertical mast bolted to the front of the chassis carries a two-piece arm linkage. The servo (mounted at the base of the mast) rotates an inner arm, which through the linkage raises or lowers a horizontal fork plate at the front. The fork is the longest moving part of any kit — between 40 and 50 mm of vertical travel.

What it teaches. Mechanical advantage, lever arms, the cost of a heavy load on a lightweight chassis (the centre of gravity shifts forward as the fork rises with cargo, lifting the rear sensors off the floor). Excellent for first-time programming of "states": pick up → drive → drop.

Calibration. Two angles to find:

  • FORK_DOWN — the angle where the fork plate sits flat on the floor, ready to slide under a pallet. Typically around 30–40°.
  • FORK_UP — the angle where the fork plate is fully raised, payload clear of the ground. Typically around 130–150°.
JavaScript · forklift cycle
const FORK_DOWN = 35
const FORK_UP   = 140

// Pick up, drive, drop, return
maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, FORK_DOWN);  basic.pause(600)
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 100); basic.pause(300)
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, FORK_UP);    basic.pause(800)
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 100); basic.pause(1500)
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, FORK_DOWN);  basic.pause(800)
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 100); basic.pause(1500)
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)

Activity ideas. Warehouse pallet sort by colour (with line sensors picking up coloured tracks); stack a tower three blocks high; race against a stopwatch to move N items between zones; "factory line" where each Maqueen has one job in a chain; combine with the GamePad for tele-operated forklift driving.

🚜 Loader — ROB0156-L · 📄 Tutorial PDF

Loader profile · arm pivots in an arc from low (scoop down) to high (carry)

What's in the box

DFRobot's official shipping list for ROB0156-L:

  • 1 × 9 g Metal Gear Servo
  • 1 × Metal Shovel (the bucket / scoop on the end of the arm)
  • 1 × Metal Servo Mounting Plate
  • 2 × M3 × 15 mm copper pillars
  • 5 × M2.5 × 5 mm screws
  • 8 × M3 × 5 mm screws
  • 1 × small Phillips screwdriver (while stocks last)

Assembly — step by step

  1. Complete the pre-flight checklist (power off, micro:bit out, servo centred at 90°, power off again).
  2. Bolt the servo to its connection plate using the two small servo-body screws.
  3. Mount the connection plate on the front-half mounting holes of the chassis top deck. The plate orientation places the servo spline at the very front of the robot.
  4. Slide the arm onto the servo spline, pointing the free end of the arm up and forward. With the servo centred at 90°, the arm should sit at roughly a 60° angle from horizontal.
  5. Secure the arm to the spline with the horn screw through the centre. Tug gently to confirm it's seated.
  6. Bolt the bucket to the free end of the arm. The bucket lip should face forward when the arm is at mid-position; the rear of the bucket should not collide with the chassis at any angle in the working range (test by hand).
  7. Connect the servo cable to S1 (brown wire toward the chassis edge).
  8. Re-insert the micro:bit, install batteries, power on.
  9. Verify the working range: servoRun(S1, 30) drops the bucket lip to the floor; servoRun(S1, 150) raises it clear of the chassis. Anywhere in between holds. If the bucket fouls the chassis at the high end, raise SCOOP_DOWN by 5° at a time.

Mechanism. Single pivoting arm at the front of the chassis with a curved metal scoop on its end. The servo rotates the arm directly — no linkage. Simpler than the forklift, with less travel but more torque per degree.

What it teaches. Single-degree-of-freedom rotation, scooping mechanics (angle of attack matters for filling vs spilling), the difference between active and passive grip — the loader can only carry what gravity holds in the bucket. Pairs naturally with sand, marbles, foam balls.

Calibration. Two angles:

  • SCOOP_DOWN — bucket lip touches the ground, ready to scoop. Around 25–35°.
  • SCOOP_UP — bucket fully raised and tilted back so cargo doesn't fall out. Around 140–160°.
JavaScript · loader scoop & carry
const SCOOP_DOWN = 30
const SCOOP_UP   = 150
const DUMP        = 40   // tip forward to release

// Drive into a pile, scoop, raise, carry, dump

maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 120); basic.pause(600)
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, SCOOP_UP);   basic.pause(700)
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 100); basic.pause(2000)
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, DUMP);       basic.pause(600)

Activity ideas. Sand-pit clearing race; "engineering rescue" — clear debris off a track; volume measurement (how many scoops to move 100 g of foam pellets); compare two student-tuned SCOOP_DOWN angles head-to-head to make calibration tangible; weight-class loading (which loads slow the robot most).

🪲 Beetle gripper — ROB0156-B · 📄 Tutorial PDF

Beetle from above · servo drives one side of the scissor; the linkage forces a mirrored second jaw

What's in the box

DFRobot's official shipping list for ROB0156-B:

  • 1 × 9 g Metal Gear Servo
  • 2 × Metal Gripper Upper Arm (the two outer jaws)
  • 1 × Metal Gripper Driven Forearm (the linkage on the passive side)
  • 1 × Metal Gripper Servo Forearm (the linkage on the servo-driven side)
  • 1 × Metal Gripper Panel
  • 1 × Metal Gripper Bottom Panel
  • 2 × M3 × 15 mm copper pillars
  • 3 × M3 × 17 mm copper pillars
  • 5 × M2.5 × 5 mm screws
  • 20 × M3 × 5 mm screws

Assembly — step by step

  1. Complete the pre-flight checklist (servo centred at 90°, power off).
  2. Bolt the servo to the front-mounting plate. The plate is shaped to extend the gripper beyond the chassis edge.
  3. Mount the plate to the chassis top deck through the front pair of M3 expansion holes. Servo spline points upward.
  4. Attach the active (driven) jaw to the servo horn. With the servo at 90°, the active jaw should point straight forward.
  5. Pin the passive jaw to its pivot point on the plate. There is one fixed pivot just opposite the servo position; the passive jaw rotates around it freely.
  6. Connect the two scissor linkage arms between the jaws. Each linkage arm crosses to the opposite jaw and pivots at both ends — the X-pattern is what couples the two jaws into mirrored motion.
  7. Test the range by hand before powering on: gently move the active jaw and confirm the passive jaw moves in the opposite direction. Both should sweep through ~50° without binding.
  8. Tighten the horn screw on the servo spline.
  9. Connect the servo cable to S1 (brown wire toward the chassis edge).
  10. Re-insert the micro:bit, batteries in, power on.
  11. Verify with deliberate angles: servoRun(S1, 30) opens the jaws wide; servoRun(S1, 110) closes them. Listen for buzzing as you close — a strained servo means the closed angle is too aggressive; back off in 5° increments until silent.

Mechanism. Two jaw arms attached to a scissor linkage. The servo rotates one jaw; the linkage forces the second jaw into a mirrored motion, so they open and close symmetrically. Range is small (typically 30–50 mm of jaw separation) but the grip is positive — the object is actively held, not just balanced.

What it teaches. Grip force vs grip width (small angle changes = big grip-pressure differences), end-effector design, the universal challenge of pick-and-place: knowing where the object is. Often paired with line sensors (drive over a colour patch, then grip).

Calibration. The most sensitive of the four kits — recalibrate per object size:

  • JAWS_OPEN — wide enough to slide over the largest object you'll lift. Around 30°.
  • JAWS_CLOSED — gripping the object firmly. Test before final code: too tight and the servo strains; too loose and the object slips.
  • Tip: if the servo buzzes when the jaws close, the angle is past the natural limit — back off by 5–10°.
JavaScript · pick & place
const JAWS_OPEN   = 30
const JAWS_CLOSED = 110   // adjust per object

function grip()    { maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, JAWS_CLOSED); basic.pause(500) }
function release() { maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, JAWS_OPEN);   basic.pause(500) }

// Approach object → grip → reverse → release
release()
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CW, 90); basic.pause(800)
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
grip()
maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.All, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 90); basic.pause(800)
maqueen.motorStop(maqueen.Motors.All)
release()

Coloured-block sort into bins; cooperative hand-off between two Maqueens (one drops, one catches); object-rescue maze (fetch the cube from inside a pen); IR-remote-driven gripper for accessibility demonstrations; "blind grip" challenge — grip without using the front camera/sensor, only line markers, to teach proprioceptive control.

🛻 Push (bulldozer) — ROB0156-P · 📄 Tutorial PDF

Push profile · the only kit where the servo drives a sensor (teal) instead of a manipulator

What's in the box

DFRobot's official shipping list for ROB0156-P:

  • 1 × 9 g Metal Gear Servo
  • 1 × Metal Trolley Plate (the front blade — passive, bolts to the chassis front)
  • 1 × Metal Pan-tilt-zoom Mount Plate (the rotating disc that carries the SR04, driven by the servo)
  • 2 × M3 × 25 mm copper pillars
  • 5 × M2.5 × 5 mm screws
  • 10 × M3 × 5 mm screws
  • 1 × 4-pin ultrasonic connecting wire (extends the SR04 cable up to the rotating mount)
  • Note: the SR04 sensor itself is sold separately for the Lite — purchase one if you don't already have it.

Assembly — step by step

  1. Complete the pre-flight checklist (servo centred at 90°, power off).
  2. Bolt the front blade to the chassis front edge. Two M3 screws into the front-most pair of expansion holes. The blade is purely passive; no wires, no servo.
  3. Move to the top deck. Bolt the servo to its connection plate.
  4. Mount the connection plate to the chassis top deck through the rear pair of M3 expansion holes — the plate places the servo near the centre/back of the chassis with the spline pointing upward.
  5. Slide the rotating sensor-mount disc onto the servo spline. With the servo at 90°, the disc's mounting slot should face forward.
  6. Tighten the horn screw through the centre of the disc.
  7. Clip the SR04 ultrasonic sensor into the bracket on the disc. The two cylindrical "eyes" of the SR04 face outward; the four pins of the SR04 face inward.
  8. Plug the SR04 into the dedicated ultrasonic socket on the chassis front — not a servo port. The Maqueen Lite has a built-in 4-pin SR04 socket; maqueen.Ultrasonic() reads from it directly without further wiring.
  9. Connect the servo cable to S1 (brown wire toward chassis edge). This drives the rotating mount only — the SR04 itself stays connected to its dedicated socket.
  10. Re-insert the micro:bit, batteries in, power on.
  11. Verify the three reference angles: servoRun(S1, 90) aims straight ahead; servoRun(S1, 20) aims hard left; servoRun(S1, 160) aims hard right. At each, take a quick distance reading let d = maqueen.Ultrasonic() with a wall ~30 cm away to confirm the sensor is alive in all positions.

Mechanism. The most distinctive of the four. Two parts:

  • A passive front blade — a vertical metal plate bolted directly to the front of the chassis. No servo. The blade pushes obstacles by virtue of the robot driving forward.
  • A servo-driven rotating mount on the top deck — this is what S1 controls. The mount holds the HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor and rotates it through 10°–170° to scan the environment.

This is the only kit where the servo drives a sensor rather than a manipulator. The student question that surfaces here: "why give the sensor its own movement?" Answer: a single ultrasonic only sees what's in front of it; sweeping turns it into a primitive radar.

What it teaches. Active sensing, polar coordinates (angle + distance = a point in 2D space), the difference between a snapshot and a sweep. With a phone or laptop receiving data over micro:bit radio, you can render a live polar plot — a real radar display.

Calibration. Three reference angles:

  • SCAN_LEFT ≈ 20° (sensor pointing hard left)
  • SCAN_CENTRE ≈ 90° (sensor straight ahead)
  • SCAN_RIGHT ≈ 160° (sensor pointing hard right)
JavaScript · sweep scanner
// Sweep the sensor and react to the closest direction
basic.forever(function () {
  let closest_dist  = 999
  let closest_angle = 90
  for (let a = 20; a <= 160; a += 20) {
    maqueen.servoRun(maqueen.Servos.S1, a)
    basic.pause(120)
    let d = maqueen.Ultrasonic()
    if (d > 0 && d < closest_dist) {
      closest_dist  = d
      closest_angle = a
    }
  }
  // React: turn toward the gap (away from closest obstacle)
  if (closest_angle < 90) {
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M1, maqueen.Dir.CW,  120)
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M2, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 120)
  } else {
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M1, maqueen.Dir.CCW, 120)
    maqueen.motorRun(maqueen.Motors.M2, maqueen.Dir.CW,  120)
  }
  basic.pause(300)
})

§ 13

AI capabilities

"AI on a Maqueen" sounds aspirational. In practice, it means three concrete things, all of which fit inside a normal classroom session.

Onboard sound classification (v2 + CreateAI)

CreateAI from the micro:bit Foundation lets students record sound samples (clapping, knocking, whistling), label them, and train a tiny model in the browser. The model exports as a MakeCode extension. The Maqueen then responds to specific sounds — "go" claps, "stop" whistles, etc. Requires micro:bit v2 (microphone).

Image classification (Teachable Machine + radio relay)

Google Teachable Machine trains an image classifier in a browser. The classifier runs on a phone or laptop pointing at flashcards. The result is sent over micro:bit radio to a Maqueen which acts on the label. The robot itself doesn't see — but it reacts to vision. Perfect for "show the green card → robot turns left".

Reinforcement learning (pure code, no extra hardware)

Q-learning and bandit algorithms fit in a few hundred lines of JavaScript. The Maqueen explores a maze, gets a "reward" when the line sensors hit the goal pattern, updates a small Q-table stored in micro:bit RAM. No camera, no neural net — but every concept that matters in modern RL.

v1 vs v2 — the AI dividing line

If you plan any AI work, buy v2 micro:bits. The v1 has neither microphone nor sufficient RAM for the new MakeCode AI runtime. Mixed classrooms are fine for non-AI activities but painful for AI ones.

§ 14

Common pitfalls

The ten things that go wrong in classrooms, in rough order of frequency:

  • Robot moves slower than yesterday → batteries are draining. Below ≈ 3.6 V the motor controller throttles. Swap cells; consider NiMH.
  • Code uploaded but nothing happens → the power switch on the chassis is off. The micro:bit is powered by USB but the motors aren't.
  • Front LEDs eat performance → at full brightness with both motors, the 4.5 V rail sags. Either dim, or accept slower top speed.
  • RGB blocks not in MakeCode → you forgot to install the neopixel extension separately. The maqueen extension does not include it.
  • Servo strips its gears → you commanded 0 or 180 with the arm assembled wrong. Always centre at 90 before mounting; respect 10–170.
  • I²C address conflict → adding HuskyLens or another I²C device clashes with the motor driver at 0x10. Check addresses before stacking.
  • Line sensors detect nothing on red surfaces → IR sensors see "black" by IR reflectance, not by visible colour. Test on actual paper before assuming colour=line.
  • Ultrasonic returns 0 or weird values → no SR04 plugged in, or it's flipped. Trig and Echo orientations matter.
  • Sound activities don't trigger → micro:bit v1, no microphone. Check the back of the micro:bit: v2 has the gold "MIC" pad and round speaker.
  • "Cannot find maqueen extension" → cached MakeCode. Hard-refresh the browser (Ctrl+Shift+R), or clear site data.
